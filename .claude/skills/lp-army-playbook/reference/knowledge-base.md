## Pool selection and screening

- [consensus] Volume divided by TVL is the single decisive pool-quality number: a small TVL facilitating large volume pays every LP well, a large TVL with little volume is a trickle. Worked examples across sessions: 10,000 TVL facilitating 700,000 volume beats 230,000 TVL facilitating 400,000; 2,500,000 TVL doing 21,000,000 of 24h volume beats 5,000,000 TVL doing 4,000,000 (fee/TVL more than 2x higher); ~34,000,000 USDof 24h volume against ~1,200,000 USDTVL (bin step 20, fee 2%) approved; ~108,000 USDof 24h fees on ~300,000 USDTVL with ~4,000,000 USDvolume = roughly 13x turnover; 7,000,000+ volume against 749 TVL preferred over 1,500,000 volume against more than 2x that TVL; a 200-bin-step pool with 232,000 USDTVL and only 1,000 USD–1,600 USDof 24h volume rejected as dead.
    (support: 9 of 14 sessions state the ratio rule; no session disputes it)
- [consensus] The baseline turnover heuristic is that 24-hour volume should exceed the pool's own TVL — that means the pool is actually turning over and generating fees.
    (support: 4 sessions state it explicitly)
- [consensus] TVL must be bounded on BOTH sides when screening. Too little TVL means adverse price moves hurt badly and you may additionally pay to initialise bins; a lot of TVL means price moves more slowly; too much TVL dilutes your fee share. Demonstrated bands: min 10,000 / max 700,000 (aggressive) and min 100,000 / max 1,000,000 (safer).
    (support: 3 sessions; the two TVL bands come from two different live screens in the same session)
- [consensus] Concrete screening filter sets demonstrated live on Metlex: (A) minimum fee 2%, 1-hour volume window, TVL 10,000–700,000, sorted by volume; (B) minimum TVL 100,000, maximum 1,000,000, all fee tiers, sorted by 1-hour volume; (C) 'Magical' screen with bin step 100–250, base fee 2%–5%, TVL 10,000–700,000, strict-list tokens only, sorted by volume-to-TVL over 1 hour; (D) base fee ≥2%, 6-hour volume window, minimum liquidity 10,000 USD; (E) base fee >2%, 6-hour volume >0 (excludes pools dead in the last six hours), then sort descending by 1-hour volume; (F) base fee 5% plus 5-minute volume >0, sorted by 5-minute volume.
    (support: 6 sessions each contributed one filter set; all follow the same shape (fee floor + short volume window + TVL bounds))
- [consensus] Higher TVL does NOT automatically mean higher fees — answered directly as false. DLMM liquidity can sit anywhere across the price range, so 200,000 of TVL may be parked far from where trades execute and earn nothing. Concentration relative to the traded price is what matters, and that is the specific difference between DLMM and other LP products.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Liquidity depth at the traded price is a hard routing constraint, not a soft preference: if only 100 USD sits in a bin, a 300 USD (or 200 USD) trade cannot fill there and Jupiter routes it elsewhere entirely. Worked comparison: with 100 USDof depth at price in one pool and 1,000 USDin another, a 600 USDtrade routes to the deeper pool while a 10 USDtrade routes to the cheaper-fee pool — trade size decides whether fee or depth dominates.
    (support: 4 sessions; the 600 USD/10 USDsplit is single-source but consistent with the rest)
- [single-source] Practical TVL floors for routing: a DLMM pool needs more than 600 USD of TVL before Jupiter sends it organic (non-arbitrage) flow, and the stated entry floor is to avoid pools under roughly 3,000–4,000 USD TVL because a dip can drop them below the 600 USD threshold. Separately, a brand-new Dynamic pool should be seeded with at least about 600 USD.
    (support: 1 session for the 600 USD routing figure and the 3,000–4,000 floor; 1 session for the 600 USD Dynamic-pool seed)
- [single-source] Undercutting on fee alone does not win routing against a deep incumbent. A pool with 730,000 TVL at 0.3% cannot be beaten by a new 0.2%-fee pool because the newcomer lacks depth to fill the large trades that matter for blue chips; a new SOL/USDC pool would need at least 100,000 of concentrated liquidity and would still usually lose; for a memecoin, once a 100-bin/2% pool holds roughly 200,000–300,000 USD TVL there is no point undercutting it.
    (support: 1 session (the pool-creation advanced session) with three separate worked figures)
- [consensus] Risk ladder for DLMM pairs, lowest to highest: JLP/USDC → SOL/USDC → utility tokens (e.g. Magic Eden) → memecoins (e.g. GRIFFAIN, FARTCOIN). The broader ladder for putting SOL to work: hardware wallet → native staking → liquid staking token (mSOL/jitoSOL) → SOL/LST or stablecoin pair → volatile pairs → memecoin pairs.
    (support: 2 sessions, mutually consistent)
- [single-source] JLP/USDC is recommended for capital preservation: JLP is less volatile than SOL so it can run a very tight range with little divergence loss. JLP receives 75% of the fees and borrow interest paid by Jupiter perp traders, was showing near 100% APY (89.58% read off the Jupiter earn screen), and behaves like an index over five assets (SOL, ETH, wBTC, USDC, USDT) with ~47% target weight to SOL and ~26% to USDC.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Fee-tier lifecycle for a new low-market-cap or meme token: start in the highest fee tier (around 5%) with a large bin step (200 or 250) so the position survives large swings and the fee cushions impermanent loss; as the token matures and TVL arrives, migrate down to lower-fee, smaller-bin-step pools to stay competitively routed. Never go so low that fee income stops covering impermanent loss.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] For stable and blue-chip pairs, TVL is the dominant selection criterion, unlike memecoins where you chase fee tiers and volume. On USDC/USDT the deepest pool (about 3,000,000 USDTVL, bin step 1, base fee 0.01%) was chosen and the ~5%-fee pools explicitly rejected, because on stables a high fee is simply never routed. The platform listed 37 USDC/USDT pools in one session and 28 in another.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Holding-period rule for choosing among many pools on the same pair: if you will leave the position alone for a day, two days or a week, pick the larger bin step and larger fee; if you will actively monitor and rebalance, pick the smallest bin step and smallest fee.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Bin step drives turnover per dollar of TVL. Observed SOL/USDC: the bin step 1 / 1 basis point pool held only ~200,000 USDbut did ~4,000,000 USDof volume, while the bin step 20 / 20 bp pool held more TVL and did roughly the same volume. BONK: a 1,100,000 USD TVL pool at bin step 25 / 0.25% fee did 2,600,000 USD of 24h volume while a bin step 8 / 0.05% pool with roughly ten times less TVL did nearly the same volume. JUP/SOL: bin step 1 / 0.1% base fee, TVL ≈59,000 USD, 24h volume >3,000,000 USD, 24h fee/TVL ≈67% (≈400 USD of fees).

- [consensus] Want two-sided swap flow: both buys and sells in the recent window mean price is oscillating through your bins and repeatedly paying you. One-sided flow means a directional move (you are only getting arbitrage fills) and is a reason to skip — or, if you expect the sell pressure to continue, an argument for entering one-sided with SOL only. Activity floors given: three swaps in the last five minutes is explicitly bad; roughly 15 to 20 trades in the last five minutes with proportionally more over the hour is worth considering.

Meteora's UI surfaces roughly the top three recommended pools per pair with a 'show all' toggle behind them. LST pools and multi-token pools can only be created by the Meteora team and carry the Meteora badge.

    (support: 1 session (advanced pool-creation))
- [single-source] Check where the trading actually happens before picking the quote asset: for BONK most trading is against SOL, so BONK/SOL (smaller fees, tighter bin steps) was preferred over BONK/USDC.
    (support: 1 session)

## Fee mechanics and dynamic fees

- [disputed] DISPUTED — how base and dynamic fee combine. Most sessions state the trader pays base fee PLUS a variable dynamic component stacked on top (e.g. 2% base at 100 bin step showing 2.5% dynamic = +0.5 percentage points). Two sessions state the opposite explicitly: the fee is never base + dynamic added together, the displayed 'dynamic fee' IS the current all-in rate and the base fee is merely the floor it can never fall below. The additive readings and the floor readings produce different expected income, so verify against the pool page before sizing.
    (support: 6 sessions describe it as additive, 2 sessions explicitly deny that and call the dynamic fee the total)
- [consensus] The total fee on every Meteora DLMM pool is hard-capped at 10%, always, with no exceptions regardless of configuration.
    (support: 6 sessions; unanimous)
- [consensus] The maximum base fee selectable at pool creation is 5%, and the base fee cannot be changed after the pool is created.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] The dynamic fee is surge pricing driven by volatility, specifically by how many bins the price crosses moving from A to B and how frequently it crosses them. Price oscillating between only two adjacent bins generally will not trigger it; a steady one-directional move across many bins does. A smaller bin step means the same price move crosses more bins and therefore earns more dynamic fee.
    (support: 8 sessions)
- [single-source] Every DLMM pool configuration (bin step + base fee combination) has its own distinct dynamic-fee formula, so the same base fee behaves differently at different bin steps. Measured ceilings: 250-bin-step / 5% base ramps to about 10% quickly; 200-bin-step / 2% base caps at 8.75%; 125-bin-step / 5% base caps at 7.68%; 100-bin-step / 2% base tops out around 3.65–3.68%; 100-bin-step / 1% base caps around 2.6–2.65% (the instructor first guessed 1.85% on air then corrected himself). Expressed as multiples: a 2% pool's dynamic fee peaks roughly 80% above base, a 1% pool's at roughly 2.5x base.
    (support: 1 session (advanced pool-creation) — the only session giving per-config ceilings)
- [consensus] The gap between the live dynamic fee and the base fee is a real-time volatility and health gauge. Rotation rule: in a 5% base-fee pool the dynamic fee needs to sit at 6% or higher (7%+ clearly healthy) to indicate organic volume — a reading of 5.11% means the pool is dead and it is time to move to the 2% pool; in a 2% pool the dynamic fee typically runs near 3.6% and when it decays to 2% you move to the 1% pool. A dynamic fee pinned at its base-fee floor is an exit/migrate signal, not something to wait out.
    (support: 3 sessions; the numeric rotation thresholds are single-source)
- [consensus] Live dynamic-fee readings used as examples: RIFF/SOL at base 2% / bin step 100 read 2.64% at entry and climbed to 3.68% as the token pumped; a 5%-base pool was charging 7% (called high); MOTHER/SOL at bin step 100 / base fee 0.8% sat only slightly above base, read as 'not pumping but not dead'; on a pair that barely moves the dynamic component sits at zero.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [disputed] Meteora's protocol fee (its cut of LP fees) is currently 0% on essentially all regular DLMM and Dynamic pools; non-zero protocol fees appear only on token launches and Alpha Vaults. One session instead states the DLMM protocol fee is 5% of the dynamic fee — treat that as the outlier. Meteora's revenue comes from lending yield on the dynamic vaults.
    (support: 4 sessions say 0%, 1 session says 5% of the dynamic fee)
- [consensus] Fees are earned per bin crossing — a fee event occurs when price moves from one bin into the next — so a smaller bin step produces more crossings and more fee events for the same price movement.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] A swap that stays entirely inside one bin executes with zero slippage and zero price impact, because all liquidity in that bin sits at a single price point. Depth illustration: a bin holding about 158 USD absorbs a 150 USD swap with no slippage, but a 300 USD swap must consume it and spill into the next bin. The bin/basket model is a port of Trader Joe's Liquidity Book.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [consensus] Routing/undercutting mechanic: among competing pools on the same pair, the lower-fee pool undercuts and captures more routed trades, but a low fee only pays if trade count is genuinely high, so there is a gradient where a high-fee pool out-earns a low-fee one. One instructor's model (stated as a guess) is that routers consume the cheapest fee tier first, then step up to the next tier, before moving to the next price point. High-fee pools (2%–5%) can sit stale and diverge from the aggregator price until an arbitrageur finds it worthwhile; degen LPs accept that leakage because the fee tier more than offsets it.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [single-source] Fee-tier ladder by token age for fresh launches: stay only in 5% base-fee pools for the first 30 minutes to 1 hour, move to 2% base-fee pools from roughly 1 hour to 12 hours of token age, and only after that consider 1% pools. Meteora-native launches carry a high base fee and typically run the dynamic fee at the 10% cap for the first hour or two.
    (support: 1 session; consistent in direction with 4 other sessions' lifecycle advice)
- [consensus] Setting a 5% base fee on a slow-moving long-term project token is a serious mistake — the pool earns almost nothing because no volume routes through such an expensive tier for a non-volatile asset. Conversely, for a genuinely stable pair the fee tier must be very low: most stablecoin volume happens at the 0.01% tier, and 1% or even 0.1% on a true stable pair performs badly.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] The lower the base fee you choose, the more impermanent-loss risk you carry, because in a one-directional decline you collect very little in fees while your inventory converts into the falling asset. Going too low wins the routing competition but loses on net.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] Depositing a single token into a bin that already holds the other token gets your deposit swapped immediately and you pay the pool's fee on that implicit swap — 2% in the demonstrated 2% pool. Check the active bin's composition first and only deposit the token that bin is already made of.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] The 'fees earned / claimed' figure on a position is stamped in USD at the moment of each claim and never re-prices afterwards: 1 SOL of fees claimed at 150 USD keeps showing 150 USD even if SOL falls to 140 USD. Unclaimed fee value, by contrast, tracks the current price in real time.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] In DLMM, fees are NOT auto-compounded — they accrue separately from the position until explicitly claimed or until a withdraw-and-close sweeps them. In Dynamic (AMM) pools the opposite is true: fees compound back into the position automatically and are never displayed, so the only way to measure income there is deposit value at entry versus withdrawal value at exit.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [single-source] Competitive framing: Orca and Raydium concentrated-liquidity pools only offer the equivalent of a spot (uniform) shape and have no dynamic fee component, so DLMM lets a smaller bag earn more through shape control plus dynamic fees, even though Raydium generally carries more TVL.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] The pool-page fee/TVL percentage is a whole-pool, whole-range figure — the demonstrated GRIFFAIN/SOL pool showed 7.43% over 24 hours against 158,000 USDTVL for liquidity spread across the entire -20% to +30% range. Concentrating near where price actually trades should beat that headline, so treat it as a floor to beat rather than a target. The displayed 24h fee/TVL metric caps at 99% on at least one tool.
    (support: 2 sessions)



## Bins, bin step and range width

- [consensus] A bin is a fixed-price container that can hold an unlimited amount of one or both tokens or nothing. Bin prices are immutable once created, exactly ONE bin is active at any moment, and only that active bin holds both tokens simultaneously — every bin above holds one token and every bin below the other. As price walks through a bin its contents flip from one token to the other.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] Bin step is expressed in basis points and defines the multiplicative price gap between adjacent bins: next bin price = current price × (1 + binStep/10000). Bin step 1 → ×1.0001, bin step 50 → ×1.005 (0.5% per bin), bin step 200 → ×1.02. Worked example: 0.01231 × 1.005 = 0.01237. One instructor misspoke the 1-bin-step multiplier as 1.001 and was corrected by the arithmetic elsewhere. Reference: 1 bp = 0.01%, 100 bp = 1%, 10,000 bp = 100%.
    (support: 6 sessions; the 1.001 misstatement is flagged in its own session)
- [consensus] A single DLMM position is capped at 69 bins — a Solana account-storage limit. Wider ranges are silently split into multiple positions: 138 bins is the maximum that still fits in two positions (139 bins → three), a 116-bin request creates two, and a 442-bin range creates seven (ceil(442/69)). The UI disables the range sliders once it splits.
    (support: 9 sessions; unanimous on the number 69)
- [consensus] Reachable range width is bin step × 69 bins: bin step 20 gives ~17%, bin step 100 gives roughly 40–49% (one session says a single 100-bin-step position spans a 40–48% price decline, another measured ~49%), bin step 250 covers an 81% price drop, bin step 400 is the maximum bin step available. A 20-bin-step pool covers a 2.5x smaller price range than a 50-bin-step pool for the same bin count.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] The base fee you pick constrains which bin steps are selectable — the two are mathematically coupled. A 0.01% base-fee pool is restricted to very small bin steps; 1%, 2% and 5% configs each have their own permitted larger bin-step lists; picking the maximum 5% base fee leaves only a restricted set with 400 the largest. You cannot pair a very low base fee with a 400 bin step.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Core trade-off: a smaller bin step packs price points closer together so price moves through them smoothly, producing more trades, more fee events, smaller per-trade price change for the trader — which is the stated reason Jupiter routing tends to favour low-bin-step pools. The cost is that price exits your range sooner and deposits fail more often on liquidity slippage. A larger bin step is safer against going out of range, is the beginner and unattended choice, and suits wild-swinging memecoins, but in a high-bin-step pool small oscillations register as no price change and produce zero volume.
    (support: 10 sessions)
- [consensus] The same range and capital can always be covered at any bin step — it just splits differently. Worked example repeated across sessions: 200 USD spanning price 169 to 169.25 sits in 2 bins of 100 USD each in a large-bin-step pool versus 8 bins of 25 USD each in a low-bin-step pool. Neither extreme wins universally.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Relative sizing of bin steps given verbally: 50 is on the small side, 100 and 200 are where you start covering a large area, 400 is the upper limit. Bin-step guidance for meme-coin positions: 25 to 80 for short-term positions, 80 to 125 for positions left open longer, since tighter bin steps require more frequent rebalancing.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Live bin-step matching procedure: measure the intended price band on the chart, then check how many bins each candidate pool fits into it. Demo A — target band ~213 to 217 on SOL/USDC: the 80 bp pool fit only 3 bins (too coarse), bin step 20 fit 11 bins (still too coarse), bin step 4 was chosen. Demo B — target range 40–43% wide: bin step 20 gave only ~17% so he switched to a 100-bin-step, 1%-base-fee pool where 69 bins covered ~49% and the whole range fit one position. Demo C — the volume-profile bounds first produced 112 bins, judged too wide on a 100 bin step, and were tightened to 51 bins.
    (support: 3 sessions, three independent worked demos)
- [consensus] Range bounds are derived from the Volume Profile Visible Range indicator: horizontal bars show traded volume per price level in the visible window and the red Point of Control marks the price where the most volume traded. Put the upper bound just above and the lower bound just below the high-volume band. The bars rescale as you zoom because they only reflect the visible time window — zoom in to exclude a launch-day spike so the node reflects recent action.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] Place the LOWER bound slightly BELOW the identified support level rather than exactly on it, because a dip to that level is expected to draw a spike of buying volume and you want liquidity already sitting underneath it. For memecoins, extend roughly 30% below the point where existing pool liquidity thins out, and set the lower edge materially below where you actually expect the bottom (a 31-to-15 range was widened down to 10 or lower) because these tokens overshoot and the flush happens on high volume when everyone else's liquidity is out of range.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [single-source] Range width should be calibrated to how far the token actually moved over the horizon you intend to leave it unattended: a measured ~20% swing over about 8 hours produced a bound 17–20% below spot as a conservative overnight choice.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Asymmetric ranges are the norm after a run-up because a breakout is more likely to retrace than continue. Demonstrated skews: about +5% up and -33% down; about +7% up (max price 0.005404) and -30% down; and an allocation skew of roughly 20% above price and 80% below, covering about 10–15% of upside versus about 50% of downside.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] On a 1-bin-step stablecoin pool go somewhat wider than the tightest possible range but not excessively wide; if you want fewer bins on a stable pair, move up to bin step 5 rather than stretching the 1-bin-step position. Demonstrated stable widths: ±4 basis points, a 5-bin curve, an 8-bin position, a 10-bin curve (6 bins considered).
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [single-source] A position can be narrowed all the way to a single bin; the stated use case is deliberately swapping one token into the other while collecting fees as price crosses that one price point — with no guarantee it fills.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] A position's bin range cannot be widened or narrowed after creation. To change the number of bins you must close and reopen, or open an additional position covering the extra range. The shape, however, CAN be changed in place by withdrawing 100% and re-adding with a different strategy, which preserves the position rent.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] General beginner rule of thumb stated outright in one session: the wider the range the better, because narrower ranges make you more likely to suffer impermanent loss. When MACD bars are small and price trends sideways you can go tight; widen when the market shifts into a visibly more volatile range.
    (support: 1 session for the 'wider is better' framing, 1 for the MACD-conditioned version)
- [consensus] Bin-step selection follows your price thesis, not a fixed rule: tight bin step if you expect the price to range, wide bin step if you expect a large move or dip. High bin steps are reserved for high-volatility tokens; too coarse and you underearn, too fine and price exits your range almost immediately.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [single-source] Stable pools are characterised by very small bin steps and low fees because risk is low — a BTC-variant stable pair's bins were about one ten-thousandth apart in value.
    (support: 1 session)



## Liquidity shapes and strategies

- [consensus] The three built-in shapes are SPOT (identical liquidity in every bin, equivalent to a plain CLMM), CURVE (concentrated in the middle of the range, thinning toward the edges) and BID-ASK (the inverse of curve — least at the current price, most at both range extremes). No mixed presets exist or are planned; the workaround is to stack shapes as separate deposits, and each shape must be its own transaction.
    (support: 11 sessions; unanimous)
- [consensus] Curve is the least-used shape and should be restricted to pairs whose price stays put — stables, or correlated/liquid-staking pairs such as SOL paired with JupSOL. Using curve on a memecoin is called a way to get badly wrecked because capital is concentrated at one price point that price may leave and never revisit. Its other legitimate uses: as an exit shape (concentrate at the price you expect to reach so you convert out efficiently), and for recompounding claimed fees you deliberately want sold quickly.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [consensus] Bid-ask is the DCA-in/DCA-out shape: it buys progressively more of the falling asset near the bottom of the range and sells progressively more into strength near the top. The rationale is that traders wait to buy at lows and sell at highs, so the extremes near support and resistance collect disproportionately more volume than the middle.
    (support: 10 sessions)
- [consensus] Bid-ask protects against divergence loss better than spot on a downside breach, contradicting the common assumption, because its volume-weighted average fill sits near the far edge of the range while spot's sits at the midpoint. Worked number: if price falls through to the bottom of the range and ranges there, a bid-ask position has lost about 25% of value where a spot position over the same range would have lost about 50%. Depositing 10 SOL into a downward one-sided bid-ask puts roughly 7 to 8 SOL into the bottom area of the range.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] The endorsed composite shape is a flat SPOT base layer across the whole range with a BID-ASK layer stacked on top of it, deposited as two successive add-liquidity calls into the same range. The spot floor guarantees fee generation at whatever bin price currently sits in — critical because Meteora's default bid-ask angle is steep and leaves very little liquidity around the current price, so a long mid-range hover leaves a large fraction of capital idle.
    (support: 8 sessions; called 'toothpaste', 'fat toothbrush' and 'the best of both worlds' in different sessions)
- [consensus] Splits used for the spot/bid-ask stack: 50/50 (0.1 SOL spot + 0.1 SOL bid-ask out of a 0.2 SOL budget; also 0.05 + 0.05); one third spot / two thirds bid-ask (1 SOL spot + 2 SOL bid-ask out of 3 SOL); 0.3 SOL spot then ~0.6 SOL bid-ask out of ~0.9 SOL; alternatives cited by other operators are 25/75 and 30/60.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Explicit rule for weighting spot versus bid-ask: when volume is heavy and dynamic fees are elevated, weight toward spot because fee income dominates; when volume is thin but price is moving a lot, weight toward bid-ask because the profit comes from positive divergence (buying low, selling high across bins) rather than fees. Do NOT use bid-ask when you expect price to range tightly around a stable level — use spot.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Single-sided SOL bid-ask below price is stated outright to be the safest DLMM strategy, and single-sided entry is the stated default of several instructors (one runs it almost exclusively). Turn OFF autofill to do it. Quote-token liquidity can only be placed at or below the current price; the active bin still ends up holding a roughly 50/50 mix of both tokens.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [consensus] THE BID-ASK FLIP (the most-repeated active strategy): open a one-sided SOL bid-ask below price so it accumulates the token as price falls; when price reaches the bottom and your SOL is fully converted, WITHDRAW the liquidity (do not close the position) and redeposit that token as a one-sided bid-ask placed ABOVE the current price so it sells progressively into the recovery. Profit is fees plus token appreciation — impermanent loss working in your favour. If price oscillates in the same band, repeat indefinitely. Worked example: a BILLY/SOL one-sided SOL bid-ask from 0.32 down to 0.20, then flipped upward.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [single-source] Aggressive variant of the flip: commit only 50% of intended capital to the initial one-sided bid-ask, and when price reaches the bottom of the range use the remaining 50% to buy the token outright, then redeposit 100% as the upward bid-ask. Only inside a pre-defined per-trade risk budget, because price can keep falling.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] THE TOOTHPASTE TUBE (attributed to geeklad / geek cloud): open with a spot base plus a bid-ask layer; when price moves into the region where your liquidity is deep, do a 100% withdraw and redeposit the same spot-base-plus-bid-ask shape re-centred on the new price, squeezing the shape along with the price. When price reverses, repeat or flip the bid-ask to the other side. The general principle: every reshape should restore a buy-low/sell-high posture.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] FEE COMPOUNDING PATTERN ('Heavy Metal Cook'): run a one-sided SOL bid-ask below price, then periodically claim accrued fees and redeposit ONLY those fees as a bid-ask layer ABOVE the current price, so the earned inventory is sold progressively into a pump without committing the principal SOL below into the token. Leave a couple of empty bins above the position specifically so the fees have somewhere to go — the empty upper space alone already means fee tokens get sold above your entry.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [consensus] BID-ASK CHILL (Mario) — the long-horizon / unattended shape: go very wide, keep only a thin spot layer near the current price, and load bid-ask on both sides so the far excursions buy low and sell high while you sleep. Worked SOL/USDC examples: a range of roughly 116 to 132 dollars with deliberately little liquidity in the 122–124 middle zone; and a range of roughly 216 down to 132 dollars per SOL read as a multi-week position, where most SOL was accumulated at 119 and below so cutting losses there costs less than a spot deposit at 123 would have.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] SOL/USDC choppy-market swing routine: with SOL oscillating between 140 and 160, place SOL as bid-ask across 140–160 selling into 160, then at 160 flip to a USDC bid-ask to buy back down. In a choppy tape this round-trips two or three times per week, earning fees plus a few dollars of spread per SOL cycled — described as one of the lower-risk strategies with decent results.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] For a large long-term SOL/USDC position: cover the entire anticipated price range (e.g. up to 250) with a wider bin step, but deliberately concentrate extra liquidity around round numbers such as 150, 160 and 170 where volatility and volume cluster. Review such pools at least weekly, ideally daily, and migrate to whichever pool is performing best. One instructor splits the layers across two DIFFERENT pools: the spot layer into a very small bin-step pool and the bid-ask layer into a 20-bin-step pool.
    (support: 1 session each for the two halves)
- [consensus] If you are bullish and already hold the token, open a one-sided position entirely ABOVE the current price: bid-ask if strongly bullish (sells more at higher prices), spot if you want more fee income while it grinds up. Demo used a +30% upper bound. Bid-ask over a wide 20–30% range is the right exit shape; but if you are confident of an immediate pump (e.g. 10%), simply holding beats a bid-ask because fees will not compensate for selling the early move cheaply.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [single-source] Ideal outcome ranking for a one-sided SOL bid-ask placed below price: (1) price dips into the range, sits there a long time accruing fees, then exits upward — you end holding only SOL plus a large fee pile earned at low prices and worth more at the higher exit; (2) dips and bounces straight back = small profit; (3) dips, stays, then breaks downward = losses cushioned by accrued fees; (4) worst case is price slicing straight through the whole range without pausing — close and either walk away or wait for a new range to form.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] One-sided deposit is the only construction that can return exactly the token amount you started with: deposit one token, let price sweep into and back out of your range, and you end with the original quantity plus fees. Any two-sided position ends at a different token mix.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] To MINIMISE divergence loss, go one-sided pure bid-ask over a wide range — no spot and no curve layers — so you accumulate only a little of the token at high prices and progressively more if it falls.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] Concentration arithmetic that drives the spot/wide trade-off: 1 SOL spread over about 5 bins gives 0.2 SOL per bin, while 2 SOL spread over a wide range gives 0.016 SOL per bin — the tight position earns far more fees per unit of volume but goes inactive the moment price leaves the range, so it demands active management.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Rule of thumb: if a range requires more than one position, use spot rather than bid-ask, because bid-ask over more than 69 bins restarts its ramp from zero on each new position (producing a sawtooth instead of a single slope) while spot tolerates the split without distortion.
    (support: 2 sessions; the sawtooth defect is demonstrated live in both)
- [single-source] Meme-to-meme pools are an automated swing trade between two volatile assets: neither leg converts to SOL, only into each other, and the two-sided volatility generates plenty of arbitrage flow. Measured roughly 4% per day on pairs of not-especially-volatile memes with a base fee around 2%; going lower risks being arbitraged out of range for little gain. Only do this if you are content ending up holding a bag of either token.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] Chief's momentum variant: in a strong uptrend, withdraw all deposited quote token and redeposit it as a CURVE with the bulk of the liquidity in the bins just under the current price, so every dip and recovery trades against a very deep bin immediately below spot.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] Bid-ask can be used purely as an ENTRY mechanism: let it fill you into the token, then withdraw and simply hold rather than redepositing — you forgo fee income but keep full upside. Recovery use: if you are underwater from a bad entry, placing a bid-ask below the bad entry price lets the bounce offset the loss.
    (support: 1 session)

    (support: 1 session, instructors disagreeing on air)

    (support: 1 session)



## Entry timing, exit rules and market reading

- [consensus] The ideal market state for an LP is a token stuck in indecision — oscillating between clear support and resistance for a long period, ideally resolving with an upward breakout — because the back-and-forth is what pays fees. The fundamental precondition is that price has to move up AND down; a pure one-way trend is not what the strategy is built for.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Set the position's lower bound at a support level and the upper bound at a resistance level; the more times price bounces between them the more fees. A level that was previously resistance and is now acting as support is a stronger boundary than one that has only ever been support once. High-volume price bands tend to sit on psychologically round numbers (the 14-day example clustered between 0.15 and 0.2), and round market-cap levels act the same way (a coin at 1,500,000 market cap would draw buyers on a dip to 1,000,000).
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] Match the chart timeframe to the intended holding period: 1-minute and 5-minute for a position you will actively re-centre or hold minutes, 15-minute and 1-hour for intraday, 6-hour or daily/weekly for overnight and multi-week positions. Check multiple timeframes to tell whether a current dump is genuinely dangerous or normal ranging. For new tokens the longer timeframes carry little information and 1m/5m are more useful.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] Chart the token on an AGGREGATED view across all venues (GMGN, or DexScreener 'all pairs') rather than the single DLMM pool, because technical analysis is an average of trader behaviour and one thin pool's chart misleads. Moves often appear in the Raydium pool first and lag into the DLMM pool, giving you time to react. In 95–98% of cases the largest-TVL pool is the Raydium pool where the token graduated — do the analysis there.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] Net volume (buys minus sells) for the chosen timeframe is the primary live signal and it precedes the chart move. Worked example: a one-minute bar of 92,000 total volume made of 38,000 buys and 54,000 sells, i.e. net -15,000. Near-zero net buy means sideways; a heavy skew means a pump or dump is coming. Screeners break this into 5-minute, 1-hour, 6-hour and 24-hour buckets with buy count, sell count, transaction count and volume — 55 buys / 43 sells in five minutes tells you the pool is generating fees where 2 buys / 9 sells does not.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Falling volume is the earliest warning that price will decline, because participants are there for quick gains and exit as activity dries up. Read several windows against each other — one example flagged only 2,300 of volume in the last 5 minutes against 36,000 in the last hour.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] MACD usage for LPs: the histogram measures net buy-versus-sell pressure around a zero line (above bullish, below bearish, distance = strength); growing bars mean strengthening momentum and shrinking/fading bars warn of reversal; the crossover of the MACD and signal lines usually occurs just BEFORE the price change, because flow turns before price does. The first bar where selling momentum stops increasing is the earliest reversal hint; two red bars of similar size mean no clear direction. This crossover is the trigger for FLIPPING the position shape, not merely closing.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] RSI is read against lines at 70, 50 and 30: above 70 overbought (do not buy, expect reversion down), below 30 oversold (consider buying, may bounce or may keep dumping), at 50 likely flat sideways. Divergence between price direction and RSI direction suggests a reversal. Use RSI with MACD rather than trusting either alone.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [single-source] Heikin Ashi candles are named among the most useful chart settings for LPing because they smooth the bars and make trend direction obvious: green candles with no lower wick = strong uptrend, red candles with no upper wick = strong downtrend, small bodies with wicks both ends = potential reversal or consolidation. A standard candle chart can look like a pump is starting where Heikin Ashi still clearly shows a strong downtrend.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Entering immediately during a large green breakout candle is an awkward, high-risk entry: you cannot know where price will settle, RSI is already above 70, and it is more likely to go down before going up. Prefer to wait; if you do enter, place a bid-ask underneath so that if price keeps rising you have not lost anything (but have not made anything either). Conversely, the best moments to OPEN are right after a pump or a dump, because volatility and volume spike and thin liquidity below a dip lets price move fast through your bins.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Pick a few indicators you understand and stick to them — stacking many mostly re-states the same information and produces analysis paralysis. The volume profile plus average volume plus support/resistance is more or less all you need. The full stack named: Heikin Ashi, volume as a coloured histogram with a moving-average overlay, Volume Profile Visible Range, MACD and RSI.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Market cap plus holder/trader count is the barometer for how reliable indicator readings are, because indicators only reflect an average. Roughly 1,000 traders or about 2,000 holders is too few for average-based prediction; tens of thousands of holders makes it workable. A token around 5,000,000 market cap can be swung by one wallet, while something like BONK moves slowly enough that no single actor dominates. The younger the token, the less reliable the chart.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Decide BOTH the loss-cut level and the take-profit level before opening; the take-profit is called equally or more important because people routinely set a stop and then round-trip their gains. Demonstrated numbers: stop at 0.145 = about 8% of downside against about 30% of upside, accepted only with small size; a stop about -30% under a range roughly 25% wide (meaningfully below the lower bound, because price can dip out of range and recover); take profit when price runs roughly 5% to 10% above the position's upper bound. One instructor cuts any position that is down 20%.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [single-source] Chief's structural stop rule (in place of a fixed percentage): read the prior trading ranges off the Volume Profile bars and quit when price crosses below the current trading range's floor, because once free-fall starts on a meme there is no way to know where it stops. When unsure, leave it, come back in a few hours, decide with new information.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Options when price leaves the range: (1) leave it and wait for price to come back — often the best move since support/resistance levels are soft and price frequently oscillates back, and appropriate if you are long-term bullish; (2) double down by opening a NEW position lower, anchored at an older support/resistance level, to keep accumulating and keep earning fees (popular but doubles the risk); (3) close and rotate, converting the divergence into a permanent loss. If you are going to cut losses, cut them quickly — otherwise be prepared for a long wait.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] 'Impermanent profit' rule: when a position has moved out of range IN YOUR FAVOUR (upward, so your base token has fully sold into the quote at progressively higher prices), take it — withdrawing crystallises the gain while sitting on it lets price round-trip and wipe it out.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Exiting at (or within about one bin of) your entry price makes realised impermanent loss essentially zero — the instructor points at the entry bin and notes withdrawing there is effectively neutral. Doing this deliberately is called very hard.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Turn your position bounds into price alerts: draw a horizontal line at the intended bound, open its settings (enable 'coordinates') to read the exact price, and paste that number into a DexScreener or DexTools alert — one for above the upper bound (take profit) and one for below the lower bound (you are losing), plus one just inside the stop as a last chance to reassess. Alerts sync to a phone via a DexScreener account logged into both web and mobile. Demonstrated alert levels in one session: 0.137, 0.0013 and roughly 0.0093.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] If you cannot monitor a position all day, LP something like SOL/USDC and check in weekly rather than chasing high-fee volatile pairs; blue-chip / strict-list tokens move much more slowly and suit unattended positions.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] For degen pools the discipline is: do not stay too long, make your money and get out, do not be greedy, use small size, and only go large once you genuinely know what you are doing.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] External non-price risk can void an otherwise good setup: one demo pool was abandoned mid-session purely because the project's Twitter account had just been hacked, on the reasoning that entering then risks being someone else's exit liquidity.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Market-depth benchmarks for judging how fast price will sweep your range: a SOL/mSOL pool with about 23,000,000 USD TVL against a ~100,000,000,000 USD Solana market cap needs on the order of 1,000,000 to 5,000,000 USD of trade to move price; a pool with TVL in the low six figures moves on trades in the thousands; at roughly 50,000 USD TVL, trades in the high hundreds of dollars move price; at around 10,000 USD TVL a few hundred dollars moves the market. Per-bin depth is the finer read — thick bins mean price moves slowly, thin bins let it whip through many bins.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Prices for the same token are never exactly equal across pools at any instant; a large trade into a thin pool shifts its price sharply and arbitrageurs pull it back. Jupiter's chosen route for the same swap changes between quote refreshes (observed going through 1DEX, then Meteora pools, then Phoenix plus a stable-weighted pool, and multi-hop routes), so a quoted venue is not stable.
    (support: 2 sessions)



## Risk, token safety and position sizing

- [consensus] Divergent loss (the instructors' preferred name for impermanent loss) is GUARANTEED whenever the relative price of the two deposited assets changes — it is continuous, starting from zero and becoming non-zero on the first swap in either direction, not an event at close. It becomes PERMANENT only when you withdraw while out of range. Divergence being present does not by itself mean the position lost value.
    (support: 9 sessions; unanimous)
- [consensus] The success test for an LP position is closing it and holding more of BOTH tokens than you deposited — fees must more than cover the divergence.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] There are exactly two independent profit engines and either alone can carry a position: (1) fee income, computed as the volume your specific liquidity captured multiplied by the effective dynamic fee, and (2) favourable/positive divergence, i.e. the pool sold what you bought at a profit. Large fees can offset selling your entry at a loss and still leave you net positive.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [consensus] Worked divergence examples, all net positive: (a) ~40 USDC plus ~0.25 SOL at a SOL price of 170 USD, exiting at 171 USD, gave up ~0.05 SOL, value 81.41 → ~81.83 USD (+0.42) plus ~0.06 USD of claimed fees; (b) the same shape stated as -0.056 SOL / +9.57 USDC, 81.60 → 81.84, divergence +0.24, fees 0.06, total +0.30; (c) opened around 169.98 with ~0.24 SOL and ~39 USDC and ended with ~0.199 SOL and ~49 USDC; (d) opened with ~40 USDC and a fraction of a SOL, closed at a SOL price of 171 holding ~0.19 SOL and ~49 USDC, net +0.48 USDincluding fees. Positive divergence is the LESS common case and shows up more in a bull market.
    (support: 4 sessions describing what appears to be the same demo position with slightly different arithmetic)
- [consensus] Pairs whose two legs track each other closely suffer far less divergent loss: stablecoin pairs (USDC/USDT), liquid-staking-token pairs, and two wrappings of the same asset (e.g. two BTC representations). Stable pools are called the safest LP play available, at the cost of much slower fee accrual.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] Pre-deposit token screen on rugcheck.xyz (reachable by clicking the token address in the Meteora DLMM UI, and now a direct button on the pool page): check whether mint authority is still enabled (supply can be inflated / settings changed), whether the token is FREEZABLE (called out as very bad — you could be unable to sell or provide liquidity and funds are stuck forever), whether liquidity is thin, whether holders are concentrated, and whether it is a copycat of a legitimate token. Because DLMM is permissionless and Meteora cannot block any pool, every safety check is the depositor's own responsibility.
    (support: 8 sessions; unanimous)
- [consensus] rugcheck alone is NOT sufficient for holder concentration because scammers split and disguise holdings; it must be paired with a cluster-analysis tool (Bubblemaps). On Bubblemaps the top 100 to 150 wallets are drawn as bubbles sized by supply share, and wallets whose rings touch or are joined by arrows have transferred tokens between them, meaning one person likely controls the cluster — a dashboard showing a 4% top holder can hide an 8 to 10% real position. The specific pattern to hunt is a cluster of wallets holding almost exactly identical percentages, which means the creator bundled. Bubblemaps only reflects the current token's transfers, does not work on every brand-new token, and creators increasingly evade it.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [disputed] Holder-concentration thresholds are stated inconsistently and should be read as a spectrum: anything over 20% in one wallet or one bundle should scare you off; a single entity or cluster around 40% can destroy a position outright; one instructor becomes uncomfortable above about 3% for a single entity, another above about 4% at meme market caps, another says 5% in one hand is already enough to do serious price damage in a single transaction and the real question is how much capital you commit. Rejected examples: a single owner holding 91% (plus low liquidity plus a copycat flag), top 10 holding 71% ('very sketchy'), top holders totalling 18% ('too high'). Accepted examples: largest holder 2% / second 1.75%, top holder 2.9% with a 2.2% whale, top 10 holding 11% or 11.89%, 15,000 and 26,000 holders.
    (support: 6 sessions give thresholds ranging from 3% to 20% for a single holder)
- [consensus] Cluster and holder-concentration analysis matters most for very young meme coins; once a token is above roughly 5,000,000 market cap it becomes much less of a practical problem, though the check is still good hygiene.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Wash-trading detection: real crypto volume never fully stops because the market runs 24 hours, so a volume histogram with repeated near-identical bursts, suspiciously uniform bar heights and outright gaps of literally zero volume is manufactured. The deployer runs bots selling to each other and buying back to climb screener rankings; when the volume abruptly dies the bot has been switched off (or run out of SOL for gas) and the rug typically follows. Fake volume can reach 200,000–300,000 USD in five minutes but is routed through the manipulator's own cheapest pools, never through Meteora, so LPing it earns nothing.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] Additional audit checklist read off GMGN: no mint function, no blacklist function, liquidity burnt or locked, top-10 holder concentration, whether the dev sold, a rug-pool check on the creator wallet's previous launches, and a 'blue chip' metric measuring the share of holders who also hold blue-chip tokens. Locked or burnt LP is a positive trust signal. A creator flagged with a 100% rug-pull history does not automatically disqualify a pool — the community may have absorbed the dumped supply — but demands extra caution, and a bad bubble map with great volume means you should NOT step down to a lower fee tier even after two or three hours.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [single-source] A full passing safety readout from one live demo: RugCheck risk analysis 'good', 34% of LP locked, a Raydium pool holding a million dollars locked, top holders 7%, an official domain; Bubblemaps largest holder 2%, second 1.75%, Raydium position 1.6%; GMGN 15,000 holders and top 10 holding 11%. Another passing readout listed no mint authority, a meaningful share of dev liquidity locked, and 100K of liquidity in one listed market.
    (support: 2 sessions each gave one full readout)
- [consensus] Position sizing: treat LPing as a separate small business with its own dedicated wallet (also required because wallet-level performance tools read the entire wallet), and commit roughly 3% of that wallet's funded balance to any single position or token. Other stated forms: risk no more than a few percent of the portfolio per position; for a first memecoin LP use 0.1 to 0.2 SOL and no more.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Scale up only on proven consistency, using a daily fee target ladder: 20 USD/day → 50 USD/day sustained for about a week → 100 USD/day, stepping back down a level whenever the target could not be sustained. Physical stress (racing heart, sweating on a price drop) is the signal the position is too large. Only increase size once the chart of your own results shows consistent wins.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Baseline expectation set for new LPs: you should expect to lose more than 50% of the time and losses typically come first; only deploy capital you can afford to lose, and expect to lose money before you make it. Profitability comes from raising the win rate and letting winners run.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] If the deposit screen warns that the pool price diverges from Jupiter's price, do NOT deposit — liquidity placed far from the true market price gets picked off by arbitrage bots. The UI warns above about 5% deviation from Jupiter's estimated market price. The pool price re-syncs on its own within a short time; wait for it.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] An empty or near-empty pool is a red flag in itself: if the pool has existed a while and holds no liquidity there is a reason, and adding to it will likely force you to buy fresh bin arrays. Meme-coin liquidity is compared to perishable produce — demand and volume can evaporate within a very short time, so such positions must not be left unattended.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] Being the very first liquidity provider on a brand-new pair carries roughly ten times the risk of entering a day after launch; the low market cap needed to be early is acceptable for outright trading but not for LPing.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] The 'burn' / memecoin-mode option on a Dynamic pool permanently locks deposited liquidity with no possibility of ever withdrawing principal — only the fees on that locked liquidity remain claimable. It is intended for token teams building trust, not for ordinary LPs.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [disputed] DISPUTED — whether to pre-compute impermanent loss: one instructor says spreadsheet/charting tools exist that plot IL against price for a given bin step and bin count and are worth digging up and using; the other says it is not worth the time because the outcome depends on how long the position stays open, the bin step, how many bins the bid-ask spans, and simultaneous moves in both the token price and the SOL price — just open the position and observe. Both agree IL magnitude is a function of bin step.
    (support: 1 session, two instructors on air)
- [consensus] Do not create new pools until you are experienced and can afford it: you eat the full bin-array creation cost, and fat-fingering the initial price (e.g. one zero too many) means selling your SOL or your token at the wrong price immediately. Creating a redundant or badly-configured pool also fragments TVL across too many venues, which hurts every LP on Meteora, and once liquidity has settled it is very hard to move because LPs act individually.
    (support: 4 sessions)



## Costs, rent and transaction settings

- [consensus] Creating one DLMM position costs Solana account rent of roughly 0.05 SOL and it IS fully refundable — you get it back when you close the position because the account is destroyed on chain. Meteora does not keep it. Variant figures quoted in different sessions: 0.05, 0.056 (7 positions = 0.395 SOL), 0.06 in one Spanish-session demo, and 0.11 SOL for two positions.
    (support: 10 sessions; unanimous on ~0.05 and on refundability)
- [consensus] Bin arrays are the on-chain accounts that must exist before liquidity can sit at a given price range. Each costs 0.07 SOL (one demo quoted 4 arrays at 0.282 SOL, i.e. ~0.0705 each) and this is NEVER refunded, because bin arrays and pools can never be destroyed on chain. You pay it only when depositing into price territory that has never held liquidity, and everyone depositing there afterwards pays only the refundable position rent.
    (support: 9 sessions; unanimous on 0.07 and non-refundability)
- [consensus] A brand-new pool with no prior liquidity requires creating a minimum of TWO bin arrays, so the first position costs 0.14 SOL of unrecoverable bin-array rent on top of position rent. Two bin arrays is also the normal count for an ordinary-width position; more only for unusually wide ranges. Worked total: about 0.197 SOL to open one first position = 0.05 refundable + 0.14 non-refundable — which exceeded the liquidity being deposited in that demo.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] Operational pre-flight: if the 'SOL needed to create position' figure shown is LARGER than 0.05 SOL, the difference is bin-array creation and is not refundable. Always click that figure to expand the itemised breakdown before signing. Instructors say support tickets about this arrive daily; the single most common cost complaint is a user who paid roughly 0.2 SOL to open a position and recovered only about 0.05 SOL on close.
    (support: 8 sessions)
- [consensus] You can detect bin-array charges in advance from the liquidity-distribution histogram under the deposit screen: if the region you are about to cover shows no existing liquidity bars at all, you are at the edge of where anyone has ever provided liquidity and will pay to initialise arrays. This happens on established pools too, simply by ranging unusually high or low.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Runaway bin-array examples to avoid: setting a lower bound to 2 USDrequired 6,932 bins spanning 101 positions at ~0.07 SOL non-refundable; stretching the lower bound to -99% produced 52 positions and roughly 3 SOL of non-refundable cost; on a bin-step-1 USDC/USDT pool a -10%/+10% range required 30 positions (~2,000 bins) and 23 brand-new bin arrays. Also: opening many separate positions instead of stacking strategies inside one accumulates rent.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [disputed] DISPUTED — pool creation cost: one session states creating a Meteora pool (Dynamic or DLMM) costs 0.03 SOL, never returned. In another session two instructors gave conflicting figures in the same breath — one said 3 SOL, the other immediately said 0.03 SOL — and the discrepancy was left unresolved on air.
    (support: 2 sessions; 0.03 SOL stated twice, 3 SOL once)
- [single-source] KNOWN BUG: opening a very tight position on bins whose arrays were rented very recently makes the UI charge bin-array rent a SECOND time. The workaround is to widen the range by one or two bins until the extra charge disappears. One instructor lost roughly 2–3 SOL to this before learning the workaround; it only shows up on extremely volatile memecoins.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Claiming fees costs a transaction fee in SOL, so on tiny accrued amounts claiming costs more than it collects — one instructor explicitly declined to claim a dust balance. Accumulated empty token accounts from closed LP positions also keep paying rent; sol-incinerator.com (by Sol Slugs) closes them and refunds it — a demo recovered about 0.01 SOL across several accounts.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Priority-fee configuration is global in the Meteora UI: pick a priority level (Ultra was used for live LPing), set either a max cap or an exact fee, and optionally route via Jito only or a mix. Demonstrated values: 0.00001 SOL (five zeros then a one) reliably lands transactions and is the recommended floor; a max cap of 0.003 SOL; an escalation ladder of about 0.0022 USD→ 0.02 USD→ 0.11 USDper transaction; one instructor observed 0.0000031 SOL per position creation with no tuning at all. Explicitly warned against: an absurd cap such as 1 SOL, which would let a single transaction spend on the order of 200 USD.
    (support: 6 sessions; the specific values differ by session and market conditions)
- [consensus] When add-liquidity or withdraw transactions repeatedly fail to land, raise the PRIORITY FEE rather than only raising slippage — this was the demonstrated fix in two separate sessions. Switching the RPC endpoint (the UI accepts a custom one; Helius named repeatedly) is the other lever and resolved a real incident. Meteora offers three built-in RPC choices plus your own, and lets you pick the block explorer (Solscan default; X-ray is dead).
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] Two independent slippage settings exist and must not be confused: SWAP slippage (for the swap widget) and LIQUIDITY slippage (tolerance for price moving between clicking deposit and the transaction landing). If deposits fail with 'exceeded bin slippage tolerance', raise LIQUIDITY slippage — the number of bins in the position is irrelevant to that error. High SWAP slippage is stated to wreck you; LP slippage is far less dangerous. Raising LP slippage can land your tokens in slightly different bins than intended. Low bin steps make deposits harder to land, so they need more of it.
    (support: 7 sessions)
- [disputed] DISPUTED — liquidity-slippage values. Stated settings, all for LIQUIDITY slippage only: heavy metal cook 4–5% in one session and ≤1.5% (up to 2% at most) in another; Chief 15%; Alex 15%; a fourth instructor 20% and sometimes 50% for memecoins; 30% used live on a quiet token; Goa argues 10–15% is required on genuinely new extremely volatile coins or you simply cannot enter; another session recommends 10–15% versus a 1% default. For stable pairs like USDC/USDT high liquidity slippage is unnecessary and irrelevant. On extremely volatile tokens the deposit price is unknowable anyway, so deliberately set very high slippage to guarantee the transaction lands rather than trying to control entry price.
    (support: 5 sessions with values from 1.5% to 50%)
- [consensus] Swap-slippage settings demonstrated: Jupiter dynamic slippage with a 3% max cap (called significant, could be tightened to 1%); about 0.1% before an LP deposit with the priority fee pushed down to around 0.0001 (which caused an expiry during congestion and had to be resent higher); the in-pool DLMM swap widget defaults to 0.5% and must be raised if used. Dial in slippage and priority fee on small test amounts (0.1 SOL) before deploying size, since a swap that fails at 0.1 SOL will also fail at full size.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Opening a DLMM position is noticeably slower than an ordinary transaction because the client must build transactions distributing liquidity across all chosen bins on-chain. The UI also refuses to deposit your entire SOL balance, enforcing a reserve of at least 0.05 SOL for gas — hitting Max is silently clamped.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Before creating a pool you should be confident that fees will cover not just impermanent loss but also the sunk, non-refundable cost of creating the pool and its bin arrays. There are cases where paying for a new bin array is acceptable and intentional — it is a judgement call, not an absolute prohibition — but the default advice for anyone inexperienced is to avoid it.
    (support: 4 sessions)



## Tools

- [consensus] Metlex / MetLex (metlex.io, built by the instructor Alex) is the main community screening and analytics tool. Tabs: Fees (per-wallet or per-pool fee accounting, paste a wallet address, read-only, no wallet connection), PnL (paste a position's opening, fee-claim or withdrawal transaction SIGNATURE — not the wallet address — and get open time, pool, price at open, fees claimed per token, profit percentage and net profit after impermanent loss), Pools (every DLMM pool for a mint with performance over 1-minute, 5-minute, 1-hour, 6-hour and 24-hour windows — the way to see sub-24h volume the Meteora UI does not show), Dex (all pools for a token across every venue with per-pool volume and buy/sell counts), DLMM/DLMM-100 (top 100 pools with filters for fee, volume window, TVL bounds, sortable by volume-to-TVL), Dynamic (top 15 meme Dynamic pools by 24h fees) and 'Magical'/'Magic' (embeds GMGN charts plus volume/TVL/today's fees per pool). ## Position management and operations

- [consensus] 'Withdraw liquidity' and 'close position' are different actions: withdrawing empties the bins but leaves the position account and its range intact and reusable (and does NOT claim fees), while 'withdraw and close position' claims outstanding fees, removes all liquidity and returns the ~0.05 SOL rent. Withdrawing 100% alone does NOT close the position — the account remains open with rent locked. The bid-ask flip and all reshaping depend on using withdraw rather than close.
    (support: 8 sessions; unanimous)
- [consensus] DLMM supports one-sided and partial withdrawals: pull an arbitrary percentage, or use the bin view to withdraw only a chosen slice of bins or only one of the two assets — e.g. shaving the top off a position and redeploying that capital in a different shape. Two caveats: the ACTIVE bin holds both tokens, so selecting it pulls out both regardless of the single-sided setting; and partial amounts are controlled by the percentage field at the top of the dialog, not by the bin-range slider. In one session the single-token withdrawal was all-or-nothing (100% of that side, no percentage control), and withdrawal percentages must be whole numbers.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [single-source] Single-sided withdrawal is the derisking primitive: as price rises, pull out just the original quote-token capital you deposited so a sudden dump cannot take your principal, and leave the rest working. Do not strip everything below price — leave one or two bins of quote liquidity underneath (Chief suggests one extra beyond that) so you still service profit-takers and keep earning.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Fees can be claimed per position or in one batch: a 'claim all fees' button harvests every open position in a pool at once on desktop, but that batch button is absent on mobile and in the Phantom in-app browser, forcing one paid transaction per position. Withdrawing and closing automatically claims fees, so no separate claim transaction is needed then.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] You may hold many independent positions in the same pool simultaneously — one instructor keeps as many as eight — using the extras to keep earning while an old out-of-range position waits for price to return rather than realising its loss. The UI sorts them by price range, highest on top.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] When the pool is TOKEN/SOL, switch the chart denomination from USD to SOL (and set the pool price display to the quote token) BEFORE transferring any range bounds — charting against USD misrepresents the pair you are market-making. Both instructors admit doing this wrong live: one had to redo an alert because he read the price in SOL but set the alert in USD; another regularly plans whole positions in USD and then wonders why the numbers do not match the pool.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Exact-price workflow for setting bounds: draw a horizontal line at the chosen level on DexScreener, open its cog/settings and enable 'coordinates' to read the exact price, then copy that number straight into the Meteora min-price / max-price field. Lock the annotation lines so they are not dragged accidentally. When typing into the Meteora range inputs you must click out of the box for the value to apply; alternatively drag the sliders on the liquidity chart.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [consensus] Range can be set three ways — sliders, an explicit bin count, or a percentage offset from the current price (e.g. 0.5% on the upside) — and the bin-count field is derived rather than directly editable. Choosing a one-sided deposit makes the UI automatically spread that single token across the entire selected range and blocks range choices invalid for that side.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Deposit sequencing for a stacked shape: turn autofill OFF, add the spot layer first and confirm it, then add the second layer as a separate bid-ask deposit into the same range. Each shape must be its own transaction.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [single-source] Entry trick to avoid paying swap fees when you need the other side: instead of swapping half your capital on an aggregator, deposit the quote half into a SINGLE bin just below the current price and wait for price to fall into it — the pool performs the swap for you and you are PAID the fee instead of paying it. It requires patience, works better in small-bin-step pools where price oscillates across bins often, and was abandoned live when price kept running away (he swapped on Jupiter instead).
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] Compounding claimed fees back into a new leg is preferred over closing and reopening a position, because closing crystallises the divergence loss you were still carrying unrealised — reopening lower after a drawdown locks it in.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] A dead or desynced pool can be fixed: Meteora's 'Sync with Jupiter's price' button appears only when there is NO liquidity sitting between the pool's current price and the true price (otherwise syncing would rip through someone's position, and the button is hidden); it also now costs a small amount whereas it used to be free. If liquidity is in the way, the manual workaround is to buy a tiny amount of the token (about 0.05 SOL worth, or a token amount plus roughly 0.01 SOL) and place it in a range covering the current active price, then swap inside the pool to walk the price to the correct level.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] When you are the only LP in a range and you are chasing a rising price with one-sided SOL, buy a very small amount of the token as well so the pool price stays synced to the market rather than drifting. After creating a volatile pool, seed the very first position with as little as 0.1 SOL purely to let the price sync — depositing 10 SOL immediately exposes you to arbitrage if the true price has already moved (a 20% gap was the example). The initial price you set does not become the real price until liquidity exists and swaps occur.
    (support: 1 session (advanced pool-creation))
- [consensus] Never make SOL the base token — SOL belongs on the quote side. Reversed base/quote creates an inverted pool whose chart reads backwards; because Meteora is permissionless, inverted pools genuinely exist for the same pair and must be checked before depositing (the correct orientation in one demo was BONK on the left, USDC on the right). Do not trust the UI's estimated market price when setting an initial pool price — read the real price on Jupiter and paste it in.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] In a pool, the base token is the one being swapped back and forth and the quote token is what prices it; on Solana the quote is almost always SOL or USDC. Report profitability in the QUOTE token, because quote-denominated results are read straight from the blockchain while USD-denominated figures depend on the Meteora API or its price oracle, which periodically mis-prices tokens or lags. SOL-denominated and USD-denominated profit can have opposite signs on the same position — one demo showed +0.006 SOL (+0.5%) that was simultaneously a loss of roughly 20 cents.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] Do not query a PnL tool with a signature that Solscan still reports only as 'Success' — wait until the transaction shows FINALIZED with max confirmations, or the tool reports a false loss. The Meteora UI's green success toast likewise does not guarantee the transaction landed.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] Swap through Jupiter, not through a pool's embedded swap widget: the in-pool widget executes against that pool only, does not route, and can give a far worse price or fail entirely if there is no liquidity at the current price point. It is acceptable only when the pool is deep (roughly 10,000,000 USDof liquidity cited) or the amount is tiny (recycling claimed fees), or when speed matters (exiting a memecoin fast) — and then its slippage must be raised above the 0.5% default. Conversely, the ONLY way to guarantee a swap pays fees to one specific pool's LPs is to use that pool's own swap function. To reduce price impact in a thin market, break the swap up or route through a deeper market — Jupiter's router splits and multi-hops (e.g. USDC→BONK→SOL) to find the best net price.
    (support: 6 sessions)
- [consensus] Slippage versus price impact: slippage is price drift between quoting and execution (quoting 170.00 USD and filling at 170.01 USD is one cent of slippage), while price impact is your own trade moving the price because the pool is thin. A 0.1 SOL swap showed under 10 basis points of price impact on a deep pair.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [consensus] MET points accrue at 1 point per 1 USDof TVL per day (sampled by snapshot at random times every 24 hours, and out-of-range liquidity STILL earns the TVL points) plus 1,000 points per 1 USDof fees earned per day, across Dynamic pools, DLMM and multi-token pools. Vault deposits, Alpha Vaults, farms and roles earn no points. Fee generation is therefore worth 1,000x more per dollar than parked TVL, and an idle large position is explicitly the wrong way to farm points.
    (support: 5 sessions; unanimous on both rates)
- [consensus] In Dynamic (non-DLMM) pools always use the BALANCED deposit option: an unbalanced deposit triggers an automatic internal swap to fit the pool's ratio and can lose money. Dynamic pools do not sit at 50/50 — one demo pool was 38% SOL / 61% vSOL, and a request to deposit 2 USDC demanded a specific matching USDT amount (~0.74) because the pool held more USDT. Dynamic pools also issue an LP token that is the SOLE receipt for the deposit — burn it, lose it or transfer it and you cannot withdraw. DLMM positions issue no LP token.
    (support: 5 sessions)



## Common pitfalls

- [consensus] The most common failure mode among participants is confusing FEES with PROFIT: earning 5% in fees does not mean 5% profit, because fees must first overcome the position's divergence loss. Profit is fees plus (or minus) the token price move.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [consensus] The 24-hour fee/TVL figure is systematically distorted because the numerator is backward-looking over a fixed 24 hours while the denominator is live. If a whale has just EXITED, TVL is understated and the yield looks better than it is; if a whale has just ENTERED (example: a 100,000 USDwhale deposit or a 50,000 USDgroup deposit into a pool that previously held 2,000 USD), TVL is overstated and the yield looks worse than reality. It is also overstated or understated whenever price is trending rather than ranging. Extreme readings: 999% displayed on a pool with 119 USDof TVL against 58,000 USDof 24-hour volume; a listing showing about 10 USDof fees earned where the liquidity had already been pulled; the metric caps at 99% on one tool. An unusually low TVL relative to recent fees is also a leading signal that whales are about to enter and dilute you.
    (support: 11 sessions; unanimous that the metric is unreliable)
- [disputed] DISPUTED — how much to trust fee/TVL: the lead instructor personally DISCOUNTS the ratio entirely and instead looks at absolute fees generated in the last 24 hours together with raw volume, then asks WHY the number looks that way; another says it is broadly reliable in deep, high-liquidity pools such as the JUP/SOL majors and should be ignored entirely in thin meme pools; a third uses it as a legitimate first-pass screen provided you cross-check absolute volume and liquidity distribution.
    (support: 3 sessions with materially different positions)
- [consensus] Clicking through the add-liquidity dialog without reading the fee breakdown is called the main way people lose money on Meteora, because non-refundable bin-array spend cannot be recovered by anyone. One instructor states he nearly lost money by not looking.
    (support: 5 sessions)
- [consensus] Bid-ask across more than 69 bins is BROKEN by the position split: each new position restarts the bid-ask ramp from zero instead of continuing the previous slope, producing a sawtooth liquidity profile. Demonstrated live — the first bid-ask ended at 0.0068 while the second restarted at 0.00003. To get a genuine wide bid-ask you must hand-calculate and place spot on the second position.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [single-source] When a range spans two positions, the two add-liquidity transactions can land PARTIALLY — one succeeded and one failed in a live demo, leaving a position with a much narrower range than intended, which then had to be withdrawn, closed, and the whole entry retried.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] The autofill field that computes the paired token amount keeps re-quoting as the market moves, so the SOL/USDC split shown before you sign is not the split you get. Autofill can be switched off and amounts entered manually — and the toggle is STICKY, carrying over to the next pool you open, so an intended balanced deposit can silently become one-sided or vice versa. The range must also be re-entered after using 'reset price' when the market walks outside the current inputs.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [single-source] lp4fun denominates results in SOL while Metlex denominates in USDC. Comparing or reporting numbers from the two without accounting for this is a source of wrong conclusions, especially when the SOL price itself has moved.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] Generic portfolio and PnL trackers such as GMGN misreport DLMM performance because they still count tokens as held after they have been deposited into a position. Use a DLMM-aware profit tool, and run LPing from a fresh dedicated wallet so the accounting stays clean.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] The Meteora API can malfunction outright: on one session day it caused PnL cards and dashboards to report large negative or missing values that did not reflect actual position state. An API outage makes monitoring tools lie; the same signature can simply be re-queried days later once it recovers.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] LPing is not staking. Depositing means your tokens WILL be swapped into the other asset as price moves — that is the mechanism working as designed, not a malfunction, and you may even want the full conversion. It is better thought of as running a market stall where low-volume 'rotten' memecoins are inventory you do not want to be holding when demand dies.
    (support: 3 sessions)
- [single-source] An asset trending DOWNWARD can still be a profitable LP position provided the downtrend is slow enough and volume high enough that accumulated fees exceed the loss in inventory value — stated as a distinctive advantage of market making over simply holding.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] The liquidity-distribution histogram under the deposit form shows where every other LP has placed capital in that pool (that pool only — not aggregated across pools or platforms). It is a useful reference for where price action is expected and where liquidity thins out, but other people's placement is not automatically the right placement for you.
    (support: 4 sessions)
- [single-source] Going two-sided by buying the volatile token on spot first is discouraged: if price keeps falling your losses compound on both the purchased bag and the LP, and fees will not cover it — hence the default of one-sided entries.
    (support: 1 session)
- [disputed] CAVEAT against the small-bin-step preference: tight bin-step pools make bid-ask and curve shapes hard to manage because the range spans multiple positions and requires manual math to stitch the shapes together, so heavy bid-ask users are better off in larger bin-step pools — an explicit trade-off against the 'tighter bin step captures more volume' rule stated earlier in the same session. One instructor rejects the 20 bin step as too tight for his taste and avoids 1 bin step entirely because tight ranges demand constant monitoring; he prefers a wider range he can set and walk away from, accepting he misses some swaps.
    (support: 2 sessions arguing against the tighter-is-better default asserted in 5 others)
- [consensus] Do not create a cheaper pool to undercut a healthy existing one — if a 5% pool is working and its dynamic fee is 7% or more, join it and add TVL; opening a lower-fee competitor splits liquidity thinly and makes neither pool profitable. A new pool only earns if other LPs join it, and they will not join an unusual configuration.
    (support: 2 sessions)
- [single-source] If price pumps while a withdrawal is being processed and the bin slider extends into the quote-token side, the withdrawal will pull quote token out of those bins too — provided the movement stays inside your slippage tolerance.
    (support: 1 session)
- [single-source] Volatility in DLMM/memecoin practice is not measured with a formal statistical model by these instructors — they read price charts, percentage moves and volume directly, while acknowledging a rigorous statistical definition exists.
    (support: 1 session)
- [consensus] Even a blue-chip-looking token can fail a rugcheck: JLP came back flagged 'danger' when checked live, which surprised the instructor. Run the check regardless of reputation. Appearing in a top-performing-pools ranking is likewise not evidence of safety.
    (support: 2 sessions)
